Questions to ask before hiring an ad agency

The sales call is the easiest time to get straight answers. Here are the questions that matter most, what good answers sound like, and the red flags that should make you walk away.

By Evan Baker · Updated Nov 28, 2026

Short answer: Before you hire an ad agency, ask who will own the ad accounts, exactly how the fee is calculated, who does the daily work, what the reports will show, how long the contract runs, and what you keep if you leave. A good agency answers all six plainly and puts them in writing. Vague answers on ownership or exit terms are the biggest red flag.

Who will own the ad accounts and the tracking?

This is the first question for a reason. If the agency builds your Google Ads account inside their own setup, or runs Meta ads from an ad account their business owns, you can lose years of data the day you part ways. That history (what keywords converted, which audiences worked, which ads won) is worth real money.

What you want to hear:

  • Google Ads: the account is yours, billed to your card, and you hold Admin access. Agencies usually link it to their manager account so they can work in it. That's normal. Just make sure you stay an Admin, since Admins control who has access.
  • Meta: the ad account, Page, pixel and datasets live in your business portfolio (Meta's name for what used to be Business Manager). The agency gets partner access. They don't own the assets.
  • Tracking: Google Analytics, Google Tag Manager and any conversion tracking are in accounts you own, with the agency added as a user.

Ask them to show you how they'll set up access before you sign. If they say "we use our own accounts for all clients," ask what happens to the account when you leave. If the answer is "it stays with us," keep looking.

How exactly is the fee calculated?

Agencies charge in a few common ways: a flat monthly fee, a percentage of ad spend, a fee per result, or a mix (a base fee plus a percentage above a spend level). None of these is wrong. What matters is that you know the number before you start and know how it changes. Our guide on how much an ad agency costs breaks the models down in more detail.

Questions to pin down:

  1. Is there a setup or onboarding fee, and what does it cover?
  2. If the fee is a percentage of spend, is there a minimum? What happens to the fee when you raise the budget?
  3. Is ad spend paid straight to Google and Meta on your card, or does it go through the agency? Paying the platforms directly is cleaner and lets you see exactly what was spent.
  4. Are creative, landing pages, video edits or extra platforms billed separately?
  5. Is there a markup on ad spend? Some agencies bill spend through their own invoices with a margin added. Ask directly.

One thing to watch with percentage-of-spend pricing: the agency earns more when you spend more, whether or not it's working. That doesn't make it a bad model, but it means you should check that their advice to raise budgets comes with numbers to back it up.

Who will actually work on my account day to day?

The person who sells you is often not the person who runs your campaigns. That's fine at a larger shop, but you should know who it is and how experienced they are.

  • Ask for the name and role of the person doing the hands-on work, and how many accounts they manage.
  • Ask whether any of the work is outsourced or white-labeled to another company. Plenty of agencies resell someone else's service. You deserve to know.
  • Ask how often someone actually works in the account. "We check it weekly" and "we make changes weekly" are different things.
  • Ask who you talk to when something breaks, and how fast they respond.

A rule of thumb we use: if they can't tell you who will be in the account and how often, assume it's a junior person with a lot of accounts and not much time for yours.

What will the reports show, and how often?

Bad reports are full of impressions, clicks and click-through rates. Those numbers matter to the person running the ads, but they don't tell you if the ads made money. Ask to see a sample report (with client names removed) before you sign.

A useful report shows:

  • Spend, leads or sales, and cost per lead or cost per sale, by platform and campaign.
  • How those numbers compare to last month and to your target.
  • What changed in the account and why.
  • What they plan to test next.

Also ask how they'll tie ad results to real revenue. If you sell through a sales team, the agency should want to know which leads closed, not just how many forms came in. An agency that only reports platform numbers is grading its own homework.

How long is the contract, and how do I get out?

Long contracts protect the agency, not you. Paid ads do need time to test and settle, usually a couple of months before you can judge results fairly. But a 12-month lock-in with no exit is a different thing.

Ask:

  • What is the minimum term, and what is the notice period to cancel?
  • Are there early-termination fees?
  • Does the contract renew automatically?
  • Is there any guarantee, and what exactly does it cover? Read the fine print. A guarantee on clicks or impressions is not the same as one on results.

Month-to-month after a short initial period is a reasonable middle ground. It gives the agency time to do the work and gives you a way out if they don't.

What happens to my creative and data if I leave?

This is the question most owners forget until it's too late. Get the answer in writing.

  • Ad creative: do you own the images, videos and copy they made for you? Can you keep running them?
  • Landing pages: if they built pages on their own software, can those pages be exported or moved to your site?
  • Tracking and audiences: will the conversion tracking, tags and saved audiences stay in your accounts?
  • Handover: will they remove their access cleanly and give you a short summary of what's running and why?

If the account, the pixel and the creative are all yours from day one, leaving is easy. That's the setup you want, even if you never plan to leave.

Red flags to watch for

Any one of these is a reason to slow down. Two or more is a reason to walk away.

  • They won't give you Admin access to your own Google Ads account or insist on running Meta from an ad account they own.
  • Guaranteed results like "page one of Google" or a fixed number of leads before they've seen your account. No one can promise that honestly.
  • Secret sauce. They won't explain what they'll do because it's "proprietary." The tactics in paid ads aren't secret. Good agencies explain their thinking.
  • No questions about your business. If they don't ask about your margins, close rate or customer value, they can't set sensible targets.
  • Reports with no money in them. Only clicks, impressions and "reach."
  • Long contracts with no exit, or auto-renewal buried in the fine print.
  • They want to start spending before tracking works. Without working conversion tracking, nobody can tell what's working.

If you're unsure whether an agency is right at all, read should I hire an ad agency or learn ads myself first.

How to use these questions

Send the questions in writing before your sales call, or ask them on the call and request a written follow-up. Compare answers side by side. Good agencies will answer quickly and plainly, and many will be glad you asked, because it means you'll be a clear-headed client.

If you already have an agency and some of these answers make you uneasy, start with access. Questions, answered

Should I own my Google Ads and Meta ad accounts if an agency runs them?

Yes. The Google Ads account should be billed to you with you as an Admin, and your Meta ad account, Page and pixel should sit in your own business portfolio with the agency added as a partner. That way you keep the history and can switch agencies without starting over.

Is a percentage-of-ad-spend fee a bad sign?

No. It's a common model. Just know the minimum, how the fee changes as spend grows, and whether there is any markup on ad spend. Since the agency earns more as you spend more, ask for numbers behind any advice to raise your budget.

How long should an ad agency contract be?

Ads usually need a couple of months of testing before you can judge them fairly, so a short initial term is reasonable. After that, month-to-month with a clear notice period is a good sign. Be wary of long lock-ins, early-termination fees and automatic renewals.

What's the biggest red flag when hiring an ad agency?

Refusing to let you own your ad accounts and tracking. If the agency keeps the accounts, you lose your data and history when you leave, which makes it hard to ever switch.
