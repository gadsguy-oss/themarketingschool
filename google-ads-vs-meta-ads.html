Google Ads vs Meta ads: which is right for your business?

Google and Meta both work. They just work for different reasons. Here's how to tell which fits your business, what each one needs from you, and when it's worth running both.

By Evan Baker · Updated Nov 28, 2026

Short answer: Google Ads captures demand that already exists: people searching for what you sell right now. Meta ads create demand by putting your offer in front of people who weren't looking yet. If customers search for your service when they need it (plumbers, lawyers, B2B software), start with Google. If your product is visual, impulse-friendly or new enough that people don't know to search for it, start with Meta. Many businesses end up running both.

What's the real difference between Google Ads and Meta ads?

The difference is intent.

On Google Search, someone types "emergency plumber near me" or "accounting software for contractors." They've told you what they want. Your ad shows up at the moment they're looking. You pay for the click, and the person on the other end already has a need.

On Meta (Facebook and Instagram), nobody is searching. People are scrolling through friends' photos and videos. Your ad interrupts that. Meta's job is to find people likely to respond based on what it knows about them, and your ad's job is to stop the scroll and make them want something they weren't thinking about a minute ago.

That one difference shapes everything else: what kind of business fits, what creative you need, how you budget and how you test.

Which businesses should start with Google Ads?

Start with Google if people already search for what you sell when they need it. Good signs:

  • Urgent or local services. Plumbers, HVAC, locksmiths, dentists, injury lawyers, roofers. When the pipe bursts, nobody waits for a Facebook ad.
  • High-ticket purchases people research. Business software, home remodeling, financial services. Buyers search and compare.
  • Known product categories. If people type the name of your product type into Google, you can be there.

The limit is search volume. If only a few hundred people a month search for your service in your area, Google can only send you so many of them, no matter how much you spend. Google's Keyword Planner gives rough search volumes so you can check before you start. Our tutorial on how to set up Google Ads covers the first campaign.

Which businesses should start with Meta ads?

Start with Meta if you need to create interest rather than catch it. Good signs:

  • Visual or impulse-friendly products. Clothing, home goods, beauty, food, gifts. A good photo or short video sells them.
  • New or unfamiliar offers. If people don't know your solution exists, they can't search for it.
  • Broad appeal. Courses, events, subscriptions, consumer apps and offers where many kinds of people could be buyers.
  • Lead generation with a strong hook. Free quotes, assessments and quizzes often work well on Meta when the offer is clear.

Meta can also work for local services, especially ones people put off (cosmetic dentistry, home upgrades, fitness). The ad gives them a reason to act now. See Meta ads for beginners for how campaigns are structured.

What creative does each platform need?

Google Search

Search ads are mostly text: headlines and descriptions that match what the person searched. The heavy lifting happens on the landing page. If someone searches "same-day crown dentist" and lands on a page that says exactly that with a clear way to book, you win. Creative needs are modest, but the page matters a lot. Other Google campaign types, like Performance Max, Demand Gen and YouTube, do need images and video, and behave more like Meta.

Meta

On Meta, creative is the targeting. Meta's automation decides who sees your ad, and the ad itself determines who stops and responds. That means you need a steady supply of images and short videos, and you need to keep testing new ones because ads wear out as the same people see them again. Plan for new creative every few weeks, not once a year. Our tutorial on Meta ads creative testing explains how to run those tests.

If you can't produce creative regularly, that alone is a reason to start with Google Search.

How do budgets and testing compare?

Clicks on Google usually cost more. As a rough guide, WordStream by LocaliQ's benchmark report on 2025 Google search data put the average cost per click a little over $5 and the average cost per lead around $70, with legal services near the top (around $8 to $9 a click) and arts and travel near the bottom (around $2 to $3). Benchmarks vary widely by industry and location, so treat them as a sanity check, not a target.

Meta clicks are often cheaper, but intent is lower, so more clicks are needed per sale. What matters is cost per lead or sale, not cost per click.

Both platforms need enough conversions to learn. Their automated bidding works best with a steady flow of conversion data. Meta's commonly cited guidance is roughly 50 optimization events in a week for an ad set to exit its learning phase. If your budget can't produce that, consolidate into fewer campaigns and ad sets, or optimize for an earlier step (like a lead form instead of a closed sale).

Testing works differently. On Google, you test keywords, ad copy and landing pages, and you learn quickly which searches convert. On Meta, you test creative and offers, and it takes more variations to find a winner. Our guide on how much to spend on ads covers testing budgets for each.

When should you run both Google and Meta?

Running both makes sense once one channel is working and you want more volume. A few patterns we see work well:

  • Meta creates demand, Google captures it. Someone sees your Meta ad, doesn't click, then searches your name on Google a week later. Your brand search campaign catches them.
  • Google hits its ceiling. You're already showing for most relevant searches and raising the budget only raises cost per lead. Meta opens up a new pool of buyers.
  • Retargeting. People who visited from Google but didn't convert can be shown Meta ads as a reminder.

What doesn't work: splitting a small budget across both from day one. Each gets too little data to learn, and you can't tell which one is helping. A rule of thumb we use: get one platform to a cost per lead or sale you're happy with, then add the second.

When you do run both, expect each platform to claim credit for some of the same sales. Judge the total by your real leads and revenue, not by adding up what each platform reports.

So which one should you pick?

  1. Do people search for what you sell when they need it? If yes, start with Google Search.
  2. Is your product visual, new, or something people buy on impulse? If yes, start with Meta.
  3. Can you produce new images and video every few weeks? If not, lean toward Google.
  4. Is search volume for your service small? If so, Google will cap out quickly. Plan for Meta as well.

Questions, answered

Is Google Ads or Meta ads better for small businesses?

It depends on how customers find you. If they search when they need you, as with most local services, Google Search usually works first. If you sell something visual or new that people don't search for, Meta usually works better. Start with one, get it working, then consider the other.

Are Meta ads cheaper than Google Ads?

Meta clicks are often cheaper, but the people clicking weren't looking for you, so it can take more clicks to get a sale. Compare cost per lead or cost per sale, not cost per click.

Can I run Google and Meta ads on a small budget?

You can, but splitting a small budget across both often leaves each without enough conversion data to learn. Most small budgets do better on one platform until results are steady.

Do I need video for Meta ads?

Not always. Strong images can work. But Meta rewards fresh creative, and short videos often perform well, so plan to make new images or videos every few weeks.
