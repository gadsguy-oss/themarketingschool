Google Ads search terms report: how to read it and what to do with it

Your keywords are what you bid on. Your search terms are what you actually paid for. This tutorial shows you how to read the report, what to act on, and how to work around the searches Google won't show you.

By Evan Baker · Updated Nov 28, 2026

Short answer: The search terms report shows the actual searches that triggered your ads, which is often very different from the keywords you chose. Sort it by cost, add irrelevant searches as negatives, add converting searches as new keywords, and do it every day or two for a new campaign and weekly after that. Google hides low-volume searches for privacy, so you'll never see every term you paid for.

What is the Google Ads search terms report?

A keyword is what you bid on. A search term is what someone actually typed before your ad showed. Because keywords match loosely, one keyword can trigger hundreds of different search terms, and some of them won't be what you meant.

The search terms report lists those searches with their impressions, clicks, cost and conversions. You'll find it in the keywords area of your account, alongside your keyword and negative keyword lists. For each search term, the report shows:

  • The search term itself.
  • Match type: how the search matched your keyword (exact, phrase, broad, or a variant of one of those).
  • Added/Excluded: whether the term is already one of your keywords or negatives.
  • Campaign and ad group the search went into, and usually the keyword that triggered it.
  • Performance columns: impressions, clicks, cost, conversions and cost per conversion.

Performance Max search terms can also appear in this report, and AI Max for Search has its own reporting that ties search terms to the ad text and landing pages shown. These features are still being rolled out, so what you see may differ by account.

How do you read the search terms report?

A long list of searches is hard to act on until you sort it. Here's how we read it:

  1. Pick a date range with enough data. For a new campaign, the last 7 days. For a mature, low-volume account, the last 30 to 90 days.
  2. Sort by cost, highest first. The top 20 rows usually hold most of your spend and most of your decisions.
  3. Add the conversions and cost per conversion columns if they aren't showing.
  4. Read each search as the customer. Ask: would someone who typed this want what my landing page offers?
  5. Check the ad group column. A good search in the wrong ad group gets the wrong ad.
  6. Then sort by clicks to catch cheap searches that add up.

Don't try to judge every row. Focus on the searches with enough spend to matter.

What should you look for in your search terms?

Every search term falls into one of a few buckets. Here's what we do with each:

What you seeExample (plumbing business)What to do
Clearly irrelevant"plumber apprentice jobs"Add the bad word (jobs) as a negative
Relevant and converting, not yet a keyword"tankless water heater repair"Add it as a phrase or exact match keyword
Relevant but in the wrong ad group"emergency plumber" in your general ad groupAdd it as a keyword in the right ad group, and a negative in the wrong one
Relevant, spending, not converting"water heater cost"Wait for more data, or check the landing page answers the question
Wrong location"plumber in [city you don't serve]"Add the place name as a negative and check your location settings
Competitor names"[competitor] plumbing reviews"Decide on purpose: block them, or move them to their own campaign
Research or DIY intent"how to fix a leaking water heater"Usually a negative for lead-gen campaigns

A rule of thumb we use for the "relevant but not converting" bucket: before you negate a relevant search, let it spend at least what you'd be happy paying for one lead. Cutting a good search after two clicks throws away traffic that may convert next week.

How often should you check the search terms report?

  • New campaign, first two weeks: every day or two. This is when the worst waste happens and the fastest wins are.
  • Weeks three to eight: weekly.
  • Stable campaign with phrase and exact match: every one to two weeks.
  • After any change that loosens matching (adding broad match, turning on AI Max, launching Performance Max): go back to every day or two for a week or so.

Small accounts can get away with a 15-minute weekly review. Put it on your calendar. The review only helps if you actually do it.

How do you turn search terms into negatives and new keywords?

Adding negatives

  1. Tick the box next to each irrelevant search term.
  2. Choose to add it as a negative keyword.
  3. Pick the level: ad group, campaign, or a shared negative keyword list. If the term is bad for every campaign, use your shared list.
  4. Edit the term down to the word that makes it wrong, and pick the match type. "plumber salary texas" becomes the broad negative salary.

For the details on match types and lists, see our tutorial on Google Ads negative keywords.

Adding new keywords

  1. Tick the converting or clearly valuable search terms that show "None" in the Added/Excluded column.
  2. Add them as keywords to the ad group that best fits, usually as phrase or exact match.
  3. If several new terms share a theme that none of your ad groups cover, build a new ad group with its own ad and landing page.

Adding a winning search term as an exact match keyword gives that search a clear home. Google gives priority to an exact match keyword that's identical to the search, over broad and phrase keywords.

Why can't you see all your search terms in Google Ads?

Google only reports a search term once it reaches enough search volume across all of Google, to protect user privacy. Searches below that threshold are rolled into a single line, often labeled "Other search terms," that shows cost and clicks but not the queries.

This is a real gap. In one independent analysis of about $20 million in spend published by Search Engine Land in 2025, hidden search terms had higher cost per click and lower click-through rates than visible ones. Results vary a lot by account, but the point holds: you are paying for searches you can't review.

How to work around it:

  • Use search terms insights on the Insights page. Google groups searches into themes, and Google says those groupings include searches too small to show individually.
  • Build a strong starter negative list before launch, since you may never see some of the bad searches.
  • Lean on phrase and exact match when budget is tight. The looser your matching, the more long-tail searches you'll get, and more of them fall under the threshold.
  • Watch the "other" line. If it's a large share of spend and conversions are weak, tighten your keywords.

A 15-minute weekly search terms routine

  1. Open the report for the last 7 days (30 if volume is low), sorted by cost.
  2. Negate anything clearly irrelevant, using the single bad word where you can.
  3. Add any converting search that isn't a keyword yet.
  4. Fix searches landing in the wrong ad group.
  5. Note relevant searches that are spending without converting, and check them again next week.
  6. Check the share of spend in the "other" line.

If your report is full of junk week after week, the problem is usually upstream, in match types, settings or tracking. Our guide to setting up a Google Ads Search campaign covers the settings that cause it most often, and signs your ads are wasting money covers what to check next.

Questions, answered

What's the difference between a keyword and a search term?

A keyword is the word or phrase you add to your campaign and bid on. A search term is the exact search someone typed that triggered your ad. One keyword can match many different search terms, especially in broad match.

Why do some search terms show as "Other"?

Google only shows search terms that reach enough search volume to protect user privacy. Searches below that threshold are grouped into a single "other" line. You can still see themes for them in search terms insights on the Insights page.

How often should I check my search terms report?

Every day or two for the first two weeks of a new campaign, then weekly. Go back to checking more often after any change that loosens matching, such as adding broad match keywords or turning on AI Max.

Should I negate search terms that got clicks but no conversions?

Only if they're irrelevant. For relevant searches, a rule of thumb we use is to let them spend about what you'd pay for one lead before cutting them. A few clicks without a conversion isn't enough data to judge.

Can I see Performance Max search terms?

Google has been adding Performance Max search terms to the standard search terms report, and you can add negatives from it. It's been rolling out gradually, so check your own account to see what's available.
