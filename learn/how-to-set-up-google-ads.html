How to set up Google Ads: your first Search campaign, step by step

This is the exact order we follow to launch a first Search campaign: tracking before spend, a small focused structure, and a week-one checklist that catches the mistakes that waste new budgets.

Get free guides by email

By Evan Baker · Updated Nov 28, 2026

Short answer: Create your Google Ads account, set up one conversion goal and test it before you spend a dollar, then build a single Search campaign for one service. Use phrase and exact match keywords in two to four tightly themed ad groups, give each ad group a responsive search ad with at least 8 to 10 distinct headlines, and set a daily budget you can afford for a full month. Check your search terms every day or two in week one.

Step 1: How do you create a Google Ads account the right way?

Before you open Google Ads, have four things ready: a landing page that matches what you sell, a short list of the services or products you want to advertise first, a monthly budget you can commit for at least 60 days, and a clear idea of what counts as a lead or sale.

  1. Use a business Google account that more than one person can recover. Losing access to the login that owns the ad account is a painful, common problem.
  2. Pick your currency carefully. You set it when you create the account and you can't change it later.
  3. Don't rush the first campaign the setup flow offers. Google's sign-up often walks you straight into creating a simplified campaign. If you want the full set of controls this tutorial uses, look for the option to skip that step or switch to the standard (expert) experience, then build your campaign there.
  4. Add billing and confirm your business details. Ads won't serve until billing is set up.

Start with one campaign. A small budget can only test one thing properly.

Step 2: How do you set up a conversion goal before launching?

A conversion is the action you want: a form submission, a phone call, a booked appointment or a purchase. Without one, Google can't tell good clicks from bad ones and neither can you.

  1. Install the Google tag on every page of your site, either directly or through Google Tag Manager.
  2. Create one primary conversion action for your main goal. For lead generation, that's usually a form submission (often tracked by a thank-you page load) plus calls from your ads.
  3. Mark everything else as secondary. Page views, scroll depth and button clicks are not leads. If they're primary, smart bidding will optimize toward them.
  4. Look at enhanced conversions. It sends hashed customer data (like an email address from your form) to improve measurement, and it's worth turning on once the basic tag works.
  5. Test it. Submit your own form and confirm the conversion shows up in Google Ads. It can take a few hours to appear.

If tracking is new to you, read conversion tracking basics before going further. This step matters more than anything else in the account.

Step 3: Which campaign settings matter for your first Search campaign?

Choose a Search campaign tied to your conversion goal. Most settings can stay at their defaults, but a few have a big effect on where your money goes.

LocationsOnly the areas you serveDon't target the whole country if you serve one city.

Step 4: How do you choose keywords and match types?

Keywords tell Google which searches you want to show up for. Match types control how loosely Google interprets them. Here's how Google currently defines them:

Match typeHow you write itWhat it can match
Broadwater heater repairSearches related to your keyword, even ones that don't contain its words. Google also uses your landing page and other keywords as signals.
Phrase"water heater repair"Searches that include the meaning of your keyword, including more specific versions.
Exact[water heater repair]Searches with the same meaning or intent as your keyword. Most control, least reach.

Google recommends pairing broad match with smart bidding, and that pairing can work well once the account has conversion data to learn from. On a brand-new account with a small budget, broad match can spend quickly on loosely related searches. A rule of thumb we use: start with phrase and exact match, then test broad later in its own ad group or campaign once you're getting conversions.

How to find your first keywords

  • Write down how a customer would describe the problem, not how you describe your service. "Water heater leaking" is a real search. "Residential hot water solutions" isn't.
  • Use Keyword Planner (inside Google Ads) to check that people actually search those terms, and to see rough cost-per-click ranges.
  • Favor keywords with buying intent: "near me," "repair," "cost," "quote," "hire," "best."
  • Skip broad, research-only terms at first, like "how does a water heater work."

Before you launch, add a starter list of negative keywords such as "jobs," "salary," "free" and "DIY," so you don't pay for searches that will never turn into customers.

Step 5: How should you organize ad groups?

An ad group is a set of closely related keywords that share the same ads and landing page. The tighter the theme, the more your ad can match what the person typed.

For a plumber, a sensible first campaign might have three ad groups:

  • Water heater repair: "water heater repair," [water heater repair near me], "water heater not working"
  • Drain cleaning: "drain cleaning," "clogged drain," [drain cleaning service]
  • Emergency plumber: "emergency plumber," "24 hour plumber," [emergency plumber near me]

Rules of thumb we use for a first campaign:

  • Two to four ad groups, each with roughly 5 to 15 keywords.
  • One theme per ad group. If you'd want a different headline for a keyword, it belongs in a different ad group.
  • Send each ad group to the most specific page you have. Someone searching for drain cleaning should land on your drain cleaning page, not your home page.

Step 6: How do you write a responsive search ad?

A responsive search ad (RSA) is the standard text ad in Search campaigns. You give Google several headlines and descriptions, and it tests combinations to show the best one for each search. The current limits:

  • Headlines: 3 minimum, up to 15, each up to 30 characters.
  • Descriptions: 2 minimum, up to 4, each up to 90 characters.
  • Display path: two optional fields, up to 15 characters each (for example, /drain-cleaning/same-day).
  • Per ad group: up to 3 enabled RSAs. One strong ad is fine to start.

Write at least 8 to 10 headlines that say different things. Mix in the keyword theme, your main benefit, proof (years in business, reviews, guarantees you actually offer), an offer, and a call to action. Ten headlines that all say "Best Plumber in Dallas" in slightly different words give Google nothing to test.

Pinning locks a headline or description into a position. Google notes it reduces the combinations available and can lower Ad Strength, so pin only what must always appear, like a required disclaimer. If you do pin, pin two or three options to the same slot.

Add assets too: sitelinks to your key service pages, callouts for things like "Free estimates," and a call asset if phone leads matter. For writing help, see how to write ad copy.

Step 7: What budget and bidding should a brand-new account use?

Budget. You set an average daily budget. On any one day Google can spend up to twice that amount, but over a month it won't charge you more than 30.4 times your daily budget. So a $50 daily budget means roughly $1,520 a month.

To sanity-check your number, take the cost-per-click estimate from Keyword Planner and divide your daily budget by it. A rule of thumb we use: if that gives you fewer than about 10 clicks a day, narrow the campaign (fewer ad groups, smaller area) rather than spreading a thin budget everywhere. For more on setting the number, read how much should I spend on ads.

Bidding. New accounts have no conversion history, so the bid strategy is a judgment call. Here's how we usually approach it:

  • If tracking is tested and working: Maximize conversions without a target CPA. It requires conversion tracking and aims to spend your full daily budget. Add a target CPA later, once you have a few weeks of conversion data and know what a lead really costs.
  • If you aren't confident in tracking yet, or the budget is very small: Maximize clicks with a maximum CPC limit, for the first couple of weeks. Then switch to Maximize conversions once conversions are recording.

Expect the first one to two weeks to be noisy while the system learns. Don't judge the campaign on day three.

What should you check in your first week of Google Ads?

Week one is about catching problems, not optimizing. Work through this list:

  1. Ad status. Every ad should be approved. Fix disapprovals the same day.
  2. Impressions. If a keyword shows "low search volume" or gets no impressions after a few days, broaden it slightly or replace it.
  3. Search terms. Open the search terms report every day or two. Add irrelevant searches as negatives. This is the single best use of your time in week one.
  4. Conversions. Confirm real leads are showing up in Google Ads and match what you see in your inbox or CRM. If a lead came in from an ad and Google didn't record it, fix tracking before anything else.
  5. Cost per click. Compare actual CPCs to your Keyword Planner estimates. If they're far higher, your budget will buy fewer clicks than you planned.
  6. Locations. Check the location report to make sure clicks are coming from the areas you serve.

Avoid big changes, like swapping bid strategies or rewriting every ad, in the first week. Small, steady fixes (negatives, disapprovals, tracking) are what get a new account to its first profitable month.

Questions, answered

How much money do I need to start Google Ads?

There's no fixed minimum, but your budget needs to buy enough clicks to learn from. Divide your daily budget by the cost-per-click estimate in Keyword Planner. If that's fewer than about 10 clicks a day, narrow your campaign to one service or a smaller area so the budget isn't spread too thin.

Should I use broad match keywords in a new account?

Google recommends broad match with smart bidding, and it can work well once you have conversion data. On a brand-new account with a small budget, we start with phrase and exact match and test broad match later, once conversions are coming in and you can see your search terms.

How many headlines should a responsive search ad have?

You can add up to 15 headlines (30 characters each) and 4 descriptions (90 characters each). Aim for at least 8 to 10 headlines that say genuinely different things: the service, a benefit, proof, an offer and a call to action.

How long before I know if my Google Ads campaign is working?

Give a new campaign at least two to four weeks before judging it, and longer on a small budget. The first week or two is noisy while bidding learns. Use that time to fix tracking, add negative keywords and clear any disapprovals.

Should I turn on Search partners and the Display Network?

For a first Search campaign, we uncheck the Display Network and usually leave Search partners off at the start too. That keeps early results limited to Google Search, so your data is easier to read. You can test Search partners later.
