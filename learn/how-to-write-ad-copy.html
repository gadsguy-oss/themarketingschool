How to write ad copy that converts

Most ad copy fails because it's vague and about the business instead of the buyer. Here's a simple framework, how to adapt it for Google and Meta, and a before-and-after rewrite you can copy.

By Evan Baker · Updated Nov 28, 2026

Short answer: Good ad copy names a specific problem, makes a clear promise, backs it with proof, and tells the reader exactly what to do next. On Google, you're answering a search in short headlines, so match the keyword and lead with the benefit. On Meta, you're interrupting a scroll, so the first line has to hook the right person before anything else matters.

What makes ad copy convert?

Ad copy has one job: get the right person to take the next step, and put off everyone else. It's not about clever lines. It's about being clear and specific enough that your ideal buyer thinks "that's me" and clicks.

Three things separate copy that converts from copy that just fills space:

  • It's about the reader, not you. "We're a family-owned company since 1998" is about you. "Same-day AC repair, or the service call is free" is about them.
  • It's specific. Numbers, names, timeframes and concrete outcomes beat adjectives every time.
  • It matches the page. If the ad promises a free quote in 60 seconds, the landing page had better deliver that. A mismatch kills conversion rates and wastes the click you paid for.

What is the best framework for writing ad copy?

The framework we use most is four parts: problem, promise, proof, call to action. You don't always need all four in one ad, especially short ones, but you should know which part each line is doing.

  1. Problem. Name the pain in the reader's words. "Tired of leads that never pick up the phone?"
  2. Promise. What changes for them. "Get booked calls, not just form fills."
  3. Proof. Why they should believe you. Real numbers, real reviews, guarantees, credentials, a demo. "4.9 stars from 600+ reviews" (only if it's true).
  4. Call to action. One clear next step. "Get your free quote," "Take the 2-minute quiz," "Start your trial."

Other frameworks like AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action) and PAS (problem, agitate, solution) work too. They all do the same thing: start where the reader is, move them to where you want them, and make the next step obvious. Pick one and use it consistently so you can compare ads fairly.

Before you write a word, answer these three questions: who exactly is this for, what do they want most, and what's stopping them from getting it today? Your copy is the bridge between those answers.

How do you write a hook that stops the scroll?

The hook is the first line of text, the words on the image, or the first few seconds of a video. If it doesn't work, nothing after it gets read.

Hook patterns that tend to work:

  • Call out the reader: "Real estate agents with fewer than 10 closings a year:"
  • Name the problem: "Your Google Ads are probably paying for searches you'd never want."
  • Specific result: "How we cut cost per lead from $80 to $35" (only with a real, documented result).
  • Contrarian take: "Stop boosting posts."
  • Question that stings: "Do you know what a customer actually costs you?"
  • Curiosity with a payoff: "The one setting that wastes most small-business ad budgets." Then actually name it.

Write at least five hooks for every ad idea. The first one you write is rarely the best. Hooks are also the cheapest, highest-impact thing to test on Meta. See Meta ads creative testing for how.

How do you make ad copy more specific?

Specific copy feels true. Vague copy feels like an ad. Go through every line and replace fuzzy words with concrete ones.

VagueSpecific
Fast serviceSame-day appointments, booked online in 2 minutes
AffordablePlans from $29 a month, cancel anytime
Trusted by manyUsed by 1,200 local restaurants
High qualitySolid oak, 10-year warranty
Learn marketingLaunch your first Google Ads campaign in a weekend

The numbers in that table are illustrations. Only use claims you can back up. Ad platforms review ads, and regulators take false or misleading claims seriously. Specific and true beats specific and invented.

How do you write Google responsive search ads?

A responsive search ad (RSA) lets you write up to 15 headlines of up to 30 characters and 4 descriptions of up to 90 characters. Google mixes and matches them, usually showing up to three headlines and two descriptions at once. Headline 1 and Description 1 usually show; the rest aren't guaranteed.

The searcher already told you what they want. Your job is to confirm you have it and give them a reason to pick you.

A simple way to fill 15 headlines:

  • 3 to 4 keyword headlines that echo the search: "Emergency Plumber in Austin"
  • 3 to 4 benefit headlines: "Arrives in 60 Minutes or Less"
  • 2 to 3 proof headlines: "Licensed and Insured Since 2010"
  • 2 to 3 offer headlines: "Free Estimates, No Call-Out Fee"
  • 2 call-to-action headlines: "Book Online in 2 Minutes"

Rules that help:

  • Make every headline work on its own and next to any other. Don't write two halves of one sentence.
  • Don't repeat the same idea in five different ways. Google values variety.
  • Use descriptions for detail: proof, offer terms, what happens after the click.
  • Pin sparingly. Pinning forces a headline into a position, which reduces combinations. Use it only for something that must always show, like a legal line.
  • Run at least two RSAs per ad group if you can. Google recommends it.

If your search terms report shows people searching for things your ads don't address, that's copy you should write next. See the Google Ads search terms report.

How do you write Meta primary text?

Meta ads have three main text fields: primary text (above the image or video), the headline (usually below it, next to the button), and an optional description. Unlike Google, nobody searched for you. You're interrupting.

  • The first line is the hook. Usually only the first line or two show before the text is cut off with a "See more" link, so put the hook and the main promise right at the top.
  • Short vs long: short primary text (one to three lines) works well for simple offers and impulse buys. Longer, story-style copy can work for higher-priced or more complex offers, where people need more convincing. Test both.
  • Write the way people talk. Short sentences. Line breaks. One idea per line. It's read on a phone.
  • Headline = the offer. Keep it short and concrete: "Free 2-minute ad quiz" or "Get 20% off your first box."
  • Make the creative and copy work together. The image or video should carry the hook visually, and the text should back it up, not repeat it word for word.

Meta lets you add several text options for one ad and can show different combinations. That's useful, but it makes it harder to know which line won. When you want a clear answer, test one variable per ad. For campaign setup basics, see Meta ads for beginners.

Before and after: rewriting a weak ad

Example: here's a made-up Meta ad for a fictional bookkeeping service for restaurants, before and after applying the framework.

Before

"At our firm, we offer high-quality, affordable bookkeeping services for businesses of all sizes. Our experienced team is dedicated to your success. Contact us today to learn more!"

What's wrong: it's about the company, it's for "businesses of all sizes" (so nobody in particular), every claim is vague, and "learn more" is a weak next step.

After

"Restaurant owners: still doing the books at midnight after close?

We handle your bookkeeping, payroll records and monthly reports, so you get your nights back and a clear profit number every month.

Built only for restaurants. Fixed monthly price. No long-term contract.

Book a free 15-minute call to see if we're a fit."

Headline: "Restaurant bookkeeping, done for you"

What changed:

  • Problem: calls out restaurant owners and a pain they'll recognize.
  • Promise: nights back and a clear monthly profit number.
  • Proof: niche focus, fixed price and no contract reduce the risk. If the business had real reviews or client numbers, they'd go here too.
  • Call to action: one clear, low-commitment step.

Write your own version of this exercise for your offer: one weak "before," one "after" using the four parts, then run both and let results decide.

Questions, answered

How long should Meta ad primary text be?

There's no single right length. Only the first line or two show before the See more link, so the hook has to be up top. Short copy often suits simple offers, while longer copy can help with expensive or complex ones. Test a short and a long version and let cost per result decide.

How many headlines should I write for a Google responsive search ad?

Aim to fill all 15 headline slots and all 4 descriptions with genuinely different messages: keyword matches, benefits, proof, offers and calls to action. Headlines can be up to 30 characters and descriptions up to 90.

Should I use AI to write ad copy?

AI tools are good for generating lots of hook and headline options quickly. Edit them hard: cut generic phrases, add specific details only you know about your offer and customers, and check every claim is true before you publish.

What's the most important part of an ad to test?

On Meta, the hook: the first line of text or the first seconds of a video. On Google, the core message in your top headlines, such as the benefit or offer. Test big differences in message before you test small wording changes.
