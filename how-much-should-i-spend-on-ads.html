How much should I spend on ads?

The right ad budget isn't a guess or a percentage. It comes from what a customer is worth to you and how many you want. Here's how to work it out, with example math you can copy.

By Evan Baker · Updated Nov 28, 2026

Short answer: Set your ad budget from your own numbers: work out what a customer is worth, decide what you can afford to pay to get one (your target CAC), use your close rate to turn that into a target cost per lead, then multiply by the number of leads you need. Start with a test budget big enough to get real data on one platform, and scale in steps once cost per customer holds. "Percent of revenue" rules are a rough starting point, not an answer.

How do I figure out how much to spend on ads?

Most owners pick a number that feels safe, like $1,000 a month, and then wait to see what happens. The problem is that the number has no link to what you need. It might be too small to learn anything, or bigger than your margins can support.

A better way is to work backward from four numbers you probably already know or can estimate:

  1. Customer value: how much gross profit a new customer brings in, over the first purchase or their lifetime.
  2. Target CAC: the most you're willing to spend on marketing to get one customer.
  3. Close rate: the share of leads that become customers.
  4. Goal: how many new customers you want per month.

Once you have those, the budget is simple math. If you're not sure how to calculate CAC, start with our tutorial on how to calculate customer acquisition cost.

Step 1: What is a customer worth to you?

Use gross profit, not revenue. A $2,000 job that costs you $1,200 in labor and materials is worth $800 to you, not $2,000. If customers come back, include what they're likely to spend over a realistic period (a year or two, not forever).

Example math (a home services company, made-up numbers):

  • Average job: $2,000
  • Gross margin: 50%, so gross profit per job is $1,000
  • Average customer books 1.5 jobs over two years
  • Customer value: $1,000 × 1.5 = $1,500 in gross profit

If you're a new business with no repeat data, use the first purchase only. It's safer.

Step 2: Set a target CAC and a target cost per lead

Your target CAC is how much of that customer value you're willing to spend to get the customer. You need to leave room for overhead and profit, so it should be well below customer value. A rule of thumb we use: start with a target CAC around a third of customer value, then adjust once you see real results. Businesses with strong repeat revenue can often go higher. Businesses that need cash fast should go lower and base it on the first sale only.

Then use your close rate to convert CAC into a target cost per lead, which is the number you'll actually watch in the ad platforms.

Example math (continued):

  • Customer value: $1,500
  • Target CAC: about a third, so $500 per customer
  • Close rate: 1 in 4 leads becomes a customer (25%)
  • Target cost per lead: $500 × 25% = $125

Notice how much the close rate matters. If this company improved its close rate to 1 in 3, the same $500 CAC would allow about $167 per lead. Fixing how fast you call leads back can be worth as much as fixing the ads.

Step 3: Turn your goal into a monthly budget

Example math (continued):

  • Goal: 10 new customers a month
  • At a 25% close rate, that takes 40 leads
  • At $125 per lead: 40 × $125 = $5,000 a month in ad spend

That's the budget your goal requires, if the ads hit the target cost per lead. Now sanity-check it. Can the business handle 40 leads a month? Can you afford to spend $5,000 for a couple of months before results settle? If not, lower the goal and the budget together rather than spending a little on everything.

For an online store, the same idea works with orders instead of leads. Example: an $80 average order with a 60% gross margin brings in $48 of gross profit. If you only count the first order, you break even at a cost per purchase of $48, so your target needs to sit below that unless customers reliably reorder.

How much should I spend to test a new platform?

The test budget isn't about making a profit in month one. It's about buying enough data to make a decision. Too small a test tells you nothing, and owners often quit a channel that would have worked.

A rule of thumb we use: plan a test that can produce at least 20 to 30 leads or sales at your target cost, run over four to eight weeks. With a $125 target cost per lead, that's roughly $2,500 to $3,750 for the test. If that's out of reach, test on one platform only, with one or two campaigns.

Google Ads testing budget

For Search, the daily budget should cover enough clicks each day to learn which keywords convert. As a rough reference, WordStream by LocaliQ's benchmark report on 2025 Google search data put the average cost per click a little over $5, with wide swings by industry (around $2 to $3 in arts and travel, and over $8 in legal). Look up typical bids for your keywords in Google's Keyword Planner, and make sure your daily budget covers at least a handful of clicks at those prices. Keep the first test to your most clearly commercial keywords so the budget isn't spread thin.

Meta ads testing budget

Meta's automated delivery needs a steady flow of conversions. Its commonly cited guidance is roughly 50 optimization events in a week for an ad set to exit the learning phase. Most small budgets can't reach that on a purchase or closed sale, so test with one campaign and few ad sets, and optimize for a lead or an earlier step if purchases are rare. Budget for several creative variations too, since on Meta the creative is what you're really testing. See Google Ads vs Meta ads if you haven't chosen a platform yet.

How do I scale an ad budget without wrecking results?

Once cost per lead or sale has held near your target for a few weeks, you can raise spend. Do it in steps.

  • Raise budgets gradually. A rule of thumb we use is increases of around 20% to 30% at a time, then wait about a week before the next one. Big jumps can push automated bidding back into a learning period and send costs up.
  • Watch the extra cost, not the average. Going from $5,000 to $7,500 a month might bring cost per lead up from $110 to $140. The average still looks fine, but the extra leads cost more than the first ones did. Make sure the extra spend still works at your target CAC.
  • Expect a ceiling. On Google Search, you eventually show for most relevant searches, and more money mostly raises costs. That's the signal to add a second platform or expand your service area or offer.
  • Keep checking closed customers. Cheap leads that never buy are not cheap. Re-check close rate as you scale.

If results get worse as you scale, also look for leaks before blaming the budget. Our guide on signs your ads are wasting money lists the usual culprits.

What about spending a percentage of revenue on marketing?

You've probably heard a rule like "spend 7% to 8% of revenue on marketing." That one is commonly credited to the U.S. Small Business Administration, as reported by the Houston Chronicle's small business section, and it comes with conditions: businesses doing under $5 million a year in sales with net profit margins around 10% to 12%.

It's a useful sanity check, but it has limits:

  • It covers all marketing, not just ad spend. Your website, staff, tools and agency fees come out of the same pot.
  • It ignores your margins and customer value. A business with high margins and repeat customers can afford much more per customer than one with thin margins and one-time sales.
  • It ignores your goals. A business trying to grow fast will spend more than one trying to hold steady.
  • It works backward from past revenue. A new business has little revenue, so the rule says spend almost nothing, which is often exactly the wrong move.

Use the percentage to check that your number isn't wildly off. Use the math above to set the actual budget.

Putting it together

Write down your customer value, target CAC, close rate and goal. Work out a target cost per lead and the monthly budget your goal requires. Run a real test on one platform, then scale in steps while watching cost per customer, not just cost per click.

Questions, answered

What is a good starting ad budget for a small business?

One that can produce at least 20 to 30 leads or sales at your target cost over four to eight weeks, on a single platform. For a business targeting $125 per lead, that's roughly $2,500 to $3,750 for the test. Smaller than that and the results are hard to read.

How do I calculate my target cost per lead?

Multiply your target customer acquisition cost by your close rate. If you're willing to pay $500 for a customer and 1 in 4 leads becomes a customer, your target cost per lead is $500 × 25% = $125.

Should I spend a percentage of revenue on advertising?

Use it only as a rough check. A commonly cited SBA guideline is 7% to 8% of revenue on all marketing for businesses under $5 million in sales with 10% to 12% net margins. Your own customer value, margins and growth goals are a better guide for ad spend.

How fast can I increase my ad budget?

Gradually. A rule of thumb we use is raising budgets around 20% to 30% at a time and waiting about a week between increases, while checking that the extra leads still come in under your target cost.
