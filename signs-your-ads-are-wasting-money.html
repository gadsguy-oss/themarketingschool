Signs your ads are wasting money

You don't need to be an ads expert to spot most wasted spend. Here are the leaks we find most often in Google and Meta accounts, and a quick check for each one you can do yourself.

Book a free account review

By Evan Baker · Updated Nov 28, 2026

Short answer: The most common signs your Google or Meta ads are wasting money are broken conversion tracking, search terms that have nothing to do with what you sell, spend leaking into Display or Audience Network placements, no negative keywords, the same people seeing the same ad over and over, and traffic sent to your homepage instead of a focused page. Each one takes about five minutes to check, and you don't need to be an expert to do it.

How do I know if my ads are wasting money?

Wasted spend rarely looks dramatic. The account runs, clicks come in, and the dashboard shows conversions. The problem is that some share of the budget is going to people who were never going to buy. Platforms are built to spend your budget. They are not built to tell you which part of it was wasted.

Below are the leaks we see most often, each with a quick self-check. Log in with read access to your Google Ads account and your Meta ad account, set the date range to the last 30 days, and work down the list. If you find two or more, it's worth fixing before you spend another dollar on growth.

Sign 1: Your conversion tracking is broken or counting the wrong thing

This is the big one, because every other decision rests on it. If tracking is off, the platform's automated bidding is optimizing toward the wrong goal, and you're judging results on bad numbers.

Common problems: a "conversion" that fires on every page view, a thank-you page that anyone can reach, calls counted when the caller hung up in three seconds, or the same lead counted twice by both a Google tag and an imported Analytics goal.

Quick self-check: pick one recent week. Count the leads or sales you actually received in your inbox, CRM or store. Compare that to the conversions Google Ads and Meta report for the same week. They will never match exactly, since platforms attribute differently. But if the ads report 60 leads and you got 15, something is wrong. Also look at which actions are counted as primary conversions. A newsletter signup should not be weighted the same as a booked call. Our tutorial on conversion tracking basics walks through how to fix it.

Sign 2: Your search terms don't match what you sell

In Google Ads, keywords are what you bid on. Search terms are what people actually typed. With broad match especially, Google can show your ad for searches that are loosely related at best. A kitchen remodeler might pay for "kitchen remodel ideas pictures" or "kitchen remodel jobs." A law firm might pay for "free legal advice."

Quick self-check: open the search terms report for your Search campaigns, sort by cost, and read the top 50. Count how many you would never want to pay for: job seekers, DIY, free, a competitor's brand you didn't mean to target, or a different service entirely. If more than a handful are clearly wrong, money is leaking. Our guide to the search terms report shows how to read it.

Sign 3: Spend is going to Display, Search partners or Audience Network

Google: Search campaigns showing on other networks

A Search campaign can also be set to show on Google's search partner sites and, through Display Expansion, on the Display Network. For some accounts these work. For many small accounts, the Display traffic in particular is cheaper clicks that rarely turn into leads.

Quick self-check: open the campaign settings and look at networks. Then view the campaign's performance segmented by network. Compare cost per conversion on Google Search versus the other networks. If the others are spending but not converting, turn them off and watch for a few weeks.

Meta: Audience Network placements

Meta's Audience Network shows your ads in third-party apps and sites rather than on Facebook, Instagram or Messenger. Clicks there can be cheap, and some are accidental. It's not always bad, but it deserves a look.

Quick self-check: in Ads Manager, break down results by placement. If Audience Network is taking spend with few or low-quality leads, exclude it. Note that Meta's controls have been changing. Third-party guides report that Meta now lets ad sets spend a small share of budget on excluded placements unless you turn that option off, and that account-level placement controls are a firmer way to block a placement. Check your settings after you exclude anything.

Sign 4: There are no negative keywords, or only a few

Negative keywords tell Google which searches you never want to show for. An account with no negatives, or a list of five words added at launch and never touched, is almost always paying for junk.

Quick self-check: look at the negative keywords on your campaigns and any shared negative lists. If there are none, or the list hasn't changed in months, that's a sign nobody is reviewing search terms. A healthy account usually adds negatives regularly, especially in the first few months. See how to use negative keywords for a starter list.

Sign 5: The same people are seeing the same ad too many times

On Meta, frequency is the average number of times each person has seen your ad. When a small audience sees the same ad again and again, results tend to slide: cost per result rises, click-through rate falls, and comments turn negative.

Quick self-check: in Ads Manager, add frequency, cost per result and click-through rate as columns and compare the last 7 days to the previous few weeks. There's no single safe number, since a retargeting audience can handle more repeats than a cold one. But if frequency is climbing while cost per result climbs with it, your audience is tired of the ad. The fix is usually new creative, not a new audience. Our tutorial on Meta ads creative testing covers how to keep fresh ads in rotation.

Sign 6: Your ads send people to the homepage

Your homepage has to serve everyone, so it's rarely the best page for someone who just clicked an ad for one specific thing. They land, don't see what they clicked for, and leave.

Quick self-check: click your own ads (or check the final URL on each one). Does each ad go to a page that matches the ad's promise, with one clear next step like "call," "book" or "get a quote"? If most ads go to the homepage, a focused landing page per service is often one of the cheapest improvements you can make.

More leaks worth checking

  • Location settings. Google's location targeting can include people who have shown interest in your area, not just people in it. A local business can end up paying for clicks from far away. Check the location report, and if you only serve locals, set targeting to people in or regularly in your area.
  • Ads running when no one can answer. If leads come by phone and calls go to voicemail after 5 p.m., look at an ad schedule.
  • Brand terms soaking up credit. If most conversions come from people searching your business name, the account may look better than it is. Look at results with brand campaigns separated out.
  • Too many campaigns for the budget. Ten campaigns on a small budget means none of them gets enough data to learn. Fewer, better-funded campaigns usually do better.

What to do if you found waste

Fix tracking first, because everything else depends on it. Then clean up search terms and negatives, tighten network and placement settings, and point ads at focused pages. Make one round of changes, then give the account a couple of weeks before judging. Changing everything every day makes it impossible to tell what worked.

Questions, answered

What's the fastest way to check if my Google Ads are wasting money?

Open the search terms report for the last 30 days, sort by cost, and read the top 50 terms. If several are clearly unrelated to what you sell, you're paying for irrelevant clicks. Add them as negative keywords.

Should I turn off the Display Network in my Search campaigns?

Check the numbers first. Segment the campaign by network and compare cost per conversion on Google Search with the other networks. If Display or search partners are spending without converting, turning them off is usually the right call for a small account.

Is Meta Audience Network bad?

Not always, but it often brings cheap, low-quality clicks. Break down your results by placement in Ads Manager. If Audience Network takes spend without producing good leads or sales, exclude it and check that the exclusion actually holds.

What is a good ad frequency on Meta?

There's no universal number. Cold audiences usually tire faster than retargeting audiences. Watch the trend: if frequency rises while cost per result rises and click-through rate falls, the audience is tired of the ad and it's time for new creative.

Why do my ad platforms report more conversions than I actually got?

Usually because the conversion action is set up wrong (firing on the wrong page, counting duplicates, or counting low-value actions as primary), or because platforms use different attribution rules. Compare a week of platform conversions to your real leads to see how big the gap is.
